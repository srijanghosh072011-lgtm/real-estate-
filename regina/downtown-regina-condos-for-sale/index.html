About Downtown Regina condos for sale

Read the reserve fund study before anything else

The reserve fund study tells you whether the building has saved enough for the roof, elevators, windows and parkade it will inevitably need. A study that says the fund is underfunded is a special assessment waiting to arrive, and it will land on you, not the seller. We read these for clients line by line during the condo document condition, and we have walked people away from units that showed beautifully.

What the condo fee should actually cover

Downtown Regina fees commonly run $300 to $500 a month for a one or two bedroom. What matters is what is inside that number: heat, water, insurance on the common property, reserve contribution, management. A low fee is not automatically good news — it often means the reserve contribution is too small, and you will pay the difference later with less warning.

The yield case for investors

A one-bedroom downtown unit currently rents in the range of $1,250 to $1,450, against purchase prices commonly between $175,000 and $230,000. That is a stronger gross yield than anything in the suburbs. Net yield is what counts, so run the real numbers: condo fee, property tax, insurance, vacancy allowance and management. We will build that model with you before you write.

Concrete versus wood frame

It is the first question to ask about any Regina condo building. Concrete construction means you hear the elevator and essentially nothing else; wood frame means you hear your neighbours. The difference shows up in resale liquidity as much as in daily comfort, and it is not always obvious from the listing photos.

Questions

Questions people ask

Are condos a good investment in Regina?

Downtown one-bedroom units produce the strongest gross rental yields in the city. The risks are special assessments from underfunded reserves and slower resale than detached housing. With the condo documents properly reviewed, they can be a sound hold.

What are condo fees in downtown Regina?

Typically $300 to $500 per month for a one or two bedroom, usually covering heat, water, common insurance, management and the reserve contribution. Confirm the inclusions in writing for each building.

What documents should I review when buying a condo?

The reserve fund study, the last two years of board minutes, the current budget and financial statements, the bylaws, the insurance certificate, and the estoppel or information certificate. Minutes are the most revealing — that is where an impending assessment gets discussed first.
